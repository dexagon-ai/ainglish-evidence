Ainglish register entry

Title: moved-earlier / moved-later — which way did the meeting move?

Form: moved-earlier / moved-later

Standard-English mapping:
Use one of the two forms instead of "moved forward", "moved back", "pushed back", "moved up", or "brought forward" when the direction of a schedule change matters. "<EVENT> is moved-earlier" means the event is rescheduled to an earlier time than its current schedule. "<EVENT> is moved-later" means the event is rescheduled to a later time than its current schedule.

Direction is judged against the event's current schedule, never against the moment of speaking: a Friday event moved-earlier to Thursday is moved-earlier even though Thursday is still in the future. The forms declare direction only. They do not state the amount or the new absolute time (state those separately when load-bearing: "standup is moved-earlier; it is now Thu 09:30Z"), do not choose a timezone, do not claim participants were notified, and do not claim the change is final — a later change can supersede it.

Bare "moved forward" and its relatives remain legal and frequency-unmarked, exactly as bare "we" remains legal beside the clusivity pair. Lossless round-trips: "the review is moved-earlier" ⇄ "the review is rescheduled to an earlier time than its current schedule"; "the freeze is moved-later" ⇄ "the freeze is rescheduled to a later time than its current schedule". Hyphen loss yields the ordinary careful-English phrases "moved earlier" and "moved later", each preserving its direction; the degraded surface can regress to a when-did-the-move-happen tense reading, which is ambiguity restored, never direction inverted.

Registered Ainglish examples:
the maintenance window is moved-earlier; it now opens Thu 02:00Z. · the ballot close is moved-later by one day; the new close is Fri 18:00Z. · standup is moved-earlier, to 09:30Z. · the freeze is moved-later; the new date follows in this thread.

Registered standard-English examples:
Ambiguous: "Next Wednesday's meeting has been moved forward two days." · Clear reading A: "…rescheduled two days earlier, to Monday." · Clear reading B: "…rescheduled two days later, to Friday." · In the published experiments, fluent readers split close to half and half between the two — each side confident, neither aware the other exists.
