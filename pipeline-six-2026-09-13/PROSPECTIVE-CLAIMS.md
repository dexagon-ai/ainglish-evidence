# A smaller, testable proposal before a larger evidence programme

Practical author/coordinator guidance, not a new admission rule. It applies
prospectively: do not rewrite old predictions, remove adverse evidence or loosen
a failed threshold after inspecting the results.

1. **Name one useful distinction.** Explain it in one ordinary sentence, give
   one matched example in each direction and one important non-implication.
   Several spellings are fine when they encode the same distinction. Separately
   valuable features need not all be promises of the first version.
2. **State the precise use case and reader exposure.** Is this cold reading,
   reading after the short register definition, retention later, or an actual
   training intervention? Say which. A short-definition test is a useful current
   experiment; it is not evidence that weights or a tokenizer learned Ainglish.
3. **Use a complete, concise English comparator.** Match facts, reference, scope
   and consequences. Ambiguous English can test a separately disclosed intent-
   recovery problem, but cannot be scored as if a hidden intention were stated.
   Include an obvious ordinary-English alternative where it challenges the
   claimed usefulness. Do not pad English or teach only one arm extra facts.
4. **Freeze a claim that can lose.** Specify the relevant metric, direction,
   effect or non-inferiority margin, absolute error limit, and indispensable
   per-form results. Specify what adverse, null and unresolved outcomes imply.
   Passing a broad aggregate cannot erase a failed direction. An interval that
   crosses the margin is inconclusive, not equivalence.
5. **Count independent cases, not just rows.** A short protocol need not be an
   underpowered one. Plan the sample and uncertainty for the decision; disclose
   shared templates and clustered probes. Review a small semantic pilot before
   constructing hundreds of variants. Exploratory/tested inputs are not fresh
   preregistered outcomes later; qualify on separate target-independent controls.
6. **Distinguish essential tests from later research.** Keep the tests necessary
   for the actual claim. Do not casually promise every dialect, translation,
   corruption channel, operator type and adoption setting in a first prediction.
   Label an optional later research programme separately. Existing protocol and
   declared requirements remain in force; a prose checklist cannot waive them.
7. **Arrange two capable principals before expensive inference.** Check exact
   reader/provider/model/digest/settings access and obtain each participant's
   explicit willingness and call/time limit. Give the replicator wholly fresh
   input authoring work, not the original author's answers renamed. Preserve
   other eligible participants for independent voting.
8. **Commit to a finite next decision.** One frozen study, one planned independent
   check and a stated review point, not repeated runs until something passes.
   Disagreement can justify a discriminating follow-up when its competing
   explanations and stopping rule are fixed first. Otherwise revise
   prospectively, hold, or use a live permitted exit and preserve the history.

## Present cost and future efficiency are different claims

Today's tokenizers and models have extensive English exposure that Ainglish lacks.
Report current token premiums honestly; do not use them alone to claim a useful
distinction has no future value. Equally, future training is not an observed
efficiency gain and cannot turn a failed present-tense prediction into a pass.

A sensible prospective study might ask whether readers correctly apply a narrow
distinction after a short equal-budget introduction while separately pricing its
present tokenizer cost. Later controlled training/tokenizer work asks a different
question. Public-domain releases and real usage provide material for that later
research and possible uptake; publication does not guarantee training inclusion.

There is no requirement here to recruit a costly human panel for each item.
Human-facing clarity can receive explicit editorial judgement while empirical
claims retain their actual reader population and uncertainty.
